# Production Line Efficiency & Downtime Analysis

In early 2022, I was engaged by a mid-sized manufacturing enterprise to address a persistent but elusive issue: their Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) metrics were stagnating, yet managers could not pinpoint why. The floor was plagued by what technicians called "micro-stops"—brief interruptions of 15 to 45 seconds that seemed insignificant in isolation but were collectively eroding throughput. While major breakdowns were well-documented, these minor stoppages lacked a clear narrative, leaving maintenance teams reacting to symptoms rather than addressing root causes. My role as a consultant was to bridge that gap by transforming raw IoT sensor data into actionable intelligence.

The project began with the ingestion of historical machine data. We had approximately eighteen months of timestamped sensor readings from three primary production lines. I wrote complex SQL queries to aggregate this data, ensuring that each downtime event was correctly categorized by line, shift, and machine ID. The raw logs were noisy, containing false positives from temperature fluctuations that did not actually halt production. Using Python and pandas, I applied statistical filters to clean the dataset, removing outliers that did not align with actual production halts. This step was crucial; without it, the subsequent analysis would have been skewed by phantom events.

Once the data was clean, I moved to the analytical phase. I used scipy to perform clustering analysis on the downtime events, grouping them by duration and frequency. The objective was to identify patterns that human operators might miss. The results were striking. Instead of a random distribution of failures, the data revealed distinct clusters. I discovered that 40% of all downtime was attributable to just three recurring mechanical issues: a specific bearing wear pattern on the conveyor system, a sensor calibration drift on the packaging unit, and a hydraulic pressure leak on the assembly arm. These were not catastrophic failures but predictable, minor degradations that accumulated over time.

To make these findings accessible to non-technical stakeholders, I built an interactive dashboard in Tableau. I knew that a static report would not drive behavior change. The dashboard was designed to show OEE trends over time, with a specific focus on the top three causes of inefficiency per shift. I used conditional formatting to highlight days where the OEE dropped below the target threshold, linking directly to the specific mechanical cause identified in the Python analysis. This allowed shift managers to see, in real-time, which subsystem was likely to cause problems based on historical patterns.

The implementation was a five-week sprint. We rolled out the dashboard to the plant floor and integrated the findings into the preventive maintenance schedule. The impact was immediate. By addressing the three identified mechanical issues proactively, the factory eliminated a significant portion of the micro-stops. Within the first month of implementation, OEE improved by six points. This was a substantial gain for a mature production line, translating directly into increased output without additional capital expenditure on new machinery.

This project reinforced the value of statistical rigor in operational contexts. It was not enough to know that machines were stopping; we had to know *how* and *why* they were stopping. By combining SQL for data extraction, Python for statistical clustering, and Tableau for visualization, we turned a vague operational headache into a clear, solvable problem. The success of this engagement led to a follow-up contract to expand the monitoring framework to two additional facilities, proving that data-driven insights can yield tangible, rapid returns in industrial settings.
