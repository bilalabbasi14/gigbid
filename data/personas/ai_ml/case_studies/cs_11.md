# Predictive Maintenance for Assembly Lines – 2024, Manufacturing  

When the client—a large manufacturing enterprise—approached us in early 2024, their biggest pain point was the unpredictable equipment failures that were grinding production to a halt and inflating their maintenance budget. As the data scientist on the ten‑week engagement, I was tasked with turning their noisy sensor streams into actionable forecasts that could keep the lines humming.  

We began by ingesting three years of high‑frequency telemetry from over 200 CNC machines. The data lived in S3 buckets and was already being logged in CSV format every second. My first step was to clean and resample the series to a 5‑minute cadence, which smoothed out spurious spikes while preserving the subtle drift patterns that often precede a failure. I used Python’s pandas and NumPy for this preprocessing, and stored the cleaned datasets back to S3 for downstream use.  

For the core predictive engine, I built an LSTM model in PyTorch, stacking two hidden layers of 128 units each and applying a dropout of 0.2 to mitigate over‑fitting. Training on a SageMaker notebook instance with a ml.p3.2xlarge GPU cut the epoch time to under four minutes, allowing us to iterate quickly. The model was trained to forecast the next 30 minutes of key vibration and temperature signals, and we evaluated it against a hold‑out set using RMSE and a custom “failure‑lead” metric that measures how early the model flags an upcoming breakdown.  

Experiment tracking was essential because we tried three different sequence lengths (30, 60, and 120 steps) and two loss functions (MSE and Huber). I logged every run with MLflow, tagging the hyper‑parameters, the dataset version, and the resulting lead time. This made it trivial to compare the 60‑step Huber configuration, which ultimately delivered a mean lead time of 22 minutes—well above the client’s 15‑minute target.  

Orchestrating the data pipeline required reliability beyond ad‑hoc scripts. I defined an Airflow DAG that ran every hour: it pulled the latest sensor files, executed the preprocessing notebook, refreshed the feature store, and triggered a SageMaker batch transform job to generate the next‑hour forecasts. Docker containers wrapped each task, ensuring the same environment ran on both our development machines and the production cluster.  

After we deployed the pipeline to the client’s AWS environment, we set up a simple dashboard in Grafana that visualized the predicted failure probability alongside the real‑time sensor readings. Within the first month of operation, the mean time between failures rose by 27%, and the client reported a $1.2 M reduction in annual maintenance costs—exactly the outcomes we had promised.  

The project concluded with a hand‑over session, detailed documentation in the repository (assembly-maintenance-forecast), and a roadmap for extending the model to other lines using transfer learning. The experience reinforced how tightly coupled robust MLOps practices must be with the underlying forecasting model to deliver tangible, bottom‑line impact in a high‑stakes manufacturing setting.
